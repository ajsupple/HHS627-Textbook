Repeated-Measures ANOVA

Up to this point, the ANOVAs we have examined have focused primarily on between-group differences in an outcome. These analyses compare mean scores across different groups of participants and are appropriate for between-subjects designs.

Another important member of the ANOVA family is the repeated-measures ANOVA (RM ANOVA).

A repeated-measures design involves collecting multiple assessments from the same participant. Rather than comparing mean scores between different groups of people, repeated-measures ANOVA evaluates whether mean scores differ within the same participants across multiple conditions or time points.

Examples from health and human sciences research include:

  • Do participants report different levels of exertion when running on a treadmill while talking with a friend, listening to music, listening to an audiobook, or listening to nothing?
  • Do participants beginning a new weight-training regimen report higher levels of self-confidence at three and six months compared with baseline?
  • Do parents who attend a divorce education program show reductions in interparental conflict from the beginning to the end of a 10-week intervention?
  • Do participants report increased fruit and vegetable consumption after attending a nutrition education program compared with their consumption before the program?

In each example, the primary research question concerns whether scores change within individuals across conditions or time points. Repeated measures are commonly used to evaluate change following an intervention, but they can also be used whenever researchers wish to compare participants’ ratings, preferences, or performance across multiple conditions.

Mixed Designs

Many studies combine repeated measures with a traditional between-subjects grouping variable. These designs are called mixed designs because they include both:

  • A within-subjects factor, representing repeated observations from the same participants.
  • A between-subjects factor, representing differences between groups of participants.

Researchers frequently use mixed designs to evaluate intervention effectiveness. In these studies:

  • One group receives a treatment or intervention that is expected to produce change.
  • A comparison or control group does not receive the intervention.
  • Participants in both groups are measured repeatedly over time.

Mixed designs allow researchers to address three questions:

  1. Within-subjects effect: Do participants change over time?
  2. Between-subjects effect: Do the groups differ overall?
  3. Interaction effect: Does the pattern of change over time differ across groups?

For example, consider a study examining a six-week strength-training program:

  • Does maximum leg-press strength increase from baseline to six weeks?
  • Do participants in the training program have greater strength overall than participants in a comparison group?
  • Does the increase in strength over time differ between the training and comparison groups?

Researchers might expect the two groups to be similar at baseline but differ after six weeks. Statistically, this prediction corresponds to an interaction between time and group membership, indicating that the amount of change over time depends on whether participants participated in the strength-training program.

Connection to the Paired-Samples t Test

The paired-samples t test evaluates whether the mean difference between two measurements equals zero. Rather than comparing two independent groups, the same participant contributes two observations.

Common examples include:

  • Pretest versus posttest
  • Before treatment versus after treatment
  • Beginning of semester versus end of semester
  • Parent report versus child report
  • Two conditions measured on the same person

For an independent-samples t test:

\[ \text{Person A is unrelated to Person B} \]

For a paired-samples t test:

\[ \text{Observation 1 and Observation 2 belong to the same person} \]

The observations are linked, or paired. Ignoring the pairing discards information and generally reduces statistical power.

Difference Scores

The paired-samples t test focuses on the difference score:

\[ D = X_1 - X_2 \]

where:

  • \(X_1\) is the first measurement.
  • \(X_2\) is the second measurement.
  • \(D\) is the difference score.

These could represent

  • Differences in participant scores before and after some experience
  • Differences in wives’ and husbands’ report of marital conflict
  • Differences in a participant’s motivation to excercise versus play video games

Null and Alternative Hypotheses

The null hypothesis is:

\[ H_0: \mu_D = 0 \]

The alternative hypothesis is:

\[ H_A: \mu_D \neq 0 \]

where \(\mu_D\) is the population mean difference.

Test Statistic

The paired-samples t statistic is:

\[ t = \frac{\bar{D}} {s_D / \sqrt{n}} \]

where:

  • \(\bar{D}\) is the mean difference score.
  • \(s_D\) is the standard deviation of the difference scores.
  • \(n\) is the number of pairs.

The formula can be interpreted as:

\[ t = \frac{\text{Signal}} {\text{Noise}} \]

The signal is the average change. The noise is the uncertainty in that change.

Degrees of Freedom

For a paired-samples t test:

\[ df = n - 1 \]

For example, if there are 30 participants:

\[ df = 29 \]

Degrees of freedom depend on the number of pairs, not the total number of observations.

Assumptions

1. Paired Observations

Scores must be meaningfully linked.

Examples include:

  • The same person measured twice
  • Married couples
  • Parent-child dyads

2. Independence Between Pairs

One participant’s scores should not influence another participant’s scores.

3. Approximate Normality of Difference Scores

The normality assumption applies to:

\[ D = X_1 - X_2 \]

The original variables do not each need to be perfectly normally distributed.

Why Is Pairing Helpful?

Suppose people differ substantially in baseline characteristics. Comparing participants with themselves removes much of this person-to-person variability.

As a result:

  • Standard errors may decrease.
  • Statistical power may increase.
  • Smaller effects may become easier to detect.

This is one reason repeated-measures designs are often efficient.

Effect Size

A common effect size for paired data is:

\[ d = \frac{\bar{D}}{s_D} \]

General guidelines are:

\(d\) Interpretation
0.20 Small
0.50 Medium
0.80 Large

Effect size should always be interpreted separately from statistical significance.

R Code

t.test(
  data$posttest,
  data$pretest,
  paired = TRUE)

Repeated-Measures ANOVA

Repeated-measures ANOVA extends the paired-samples t test to situations involving three or more repeated conditions or time points. A two-level repeated-measures ANOVA is mathematically equivalent to a paired-samples t test.

A mixed-design ANOVA additionally includes one or more between-subjects factors.

Example 1: One-Factor Repeated-Measures ANOVA

Research Scenario

Suppose exercise researchers are interested in whether different auditory distractions influence perceived exertion during exercise.

Participants complete a 20-minute treadmill run under four conditions:

  • Talking with a friend
  • Listening to music
  • Listening to an audiobook
  • No audio stimulus

After each run, participants rate their perceived exertion on a scale ranging from 1 to 20.

Why Use a Repeated-Measures ANOVA?

A traditional one-way ANOVA would not be appropriate because the observations are not independent. Each participant contributes four observations, one for each listening condition.

A repeated-measures ANOVA is appropriate because:

  • The same participants experience all four conditions.
  • Each participant serves as their own control.
  • Stable individual differences can be separated from the within-person error term.
  • The analysis evaluates whether mean exertion differs across listening conditions.

The within-subjects factor is condition, with four levels:

  • Talking
  • Music
  • Audiobook
  • Silence

The outcome variable is perceived exertion.

The simulation includes a participant-level effect so that scores from the same participant are positively correlated across conditions.

Conduct the Repeated-Measures ANOVA

model_exer <- aov(

NoteWhat does Error(id / condition) mean?

Repeated-measures data contain observations that are related because they come from the same participant. We therefore need to tell R that the four condition scores for a given participant should not be treated as independent observations.

The expression Error(id / condition) specifies the error structure for the repeated-measures ANOVA. It tells aov() to account for variability associated with participants and with participant-by-condition differences when testing the effect of condition.

Example 2: Mixed-Design ANOVA

Research Scenario

Researchers develop a six-week strength-training intervention and wish to determine whether participation in the program is associated with greater improvement in leg-press strength than participation in a comparison group. Untrained athletes who currently do not lift weights were first recruited and then randomly assigned to one of two conditions: the strength training program and a control group. The experimental group was led by a personal trainer and coached on how to properly work to muscle failure with proper form. The control group was asked to work on their leg press strength by going to the recreation center at a university. Participants in both conditions were rewarded with $10 each time they “swiped in” at the student recreation center using their university provided ID.

Participants are assessed at:

  • Baseline
  • Six weeks

Participants belong to one of two groups:

  • Training program
  • Control group

Design

The repeated-measures factor is time, with two levels:

  • Baseline
  • Six weeks

The between-subjects factor is group, with two levels:

  • Training
  • Control

A mixed-design ANOVA addresses three questions:

  1. Do participants change over time?
  2. Do groups differ overall?
  3. Does the amount of change over time differ between groups?

The third question is usually the primary intervention question because it evaluates whether improvement differs between the training and control groups.

Conduct the Mixed-Design ANOVA using the afex package

NoteThe ‘afex’ package for RM-ANOVAs

afex::aov_ez() is designed for repeated-measures and mixed-design ANOVA. By default, it uses Type III sums of squares and sum-to-zero coding for categorical predictors. This is a common choice for factorial and mixed designs, especially when the design is not perfectly balanced. In balanced designs, Type II and Type III tests are often similar; in unbalanced designs, the choice of SS type can affect the results, so it should be reported clearly.

afex is also useful because it can:

  • Report appropriate corrections for violations of sphericity. (see below)
  • Provide Greenhouse-Geisser and Huynh-Feldt adjusted results when needed. (see below)
  • Report effect sizes such as generalized eta squared.
  • Handle complex repeated-measures error structures more transparently than a manually specified aov() model.
  • Work well with emmeans() for follow-up comparisons.
library(afex)

model_strength <- aov_ez(

Repeated-Measures ANOVA with More Than Two Time Points

Many studies include more than two measurement occasions. These studies may involve interventions related to health, nutrition, exercise, parenting, or education. They may also examine developmental change and whether that change differs across groups.

Traditional repeated-measures ANOVA can be useful for simple, balanced designs. However, many contemporary researchers use linear mixed models for more complex longitudinal data. Linear mixed models can:

  • Accommodate some incomplete data under assumptions about the missing-data mechanism.
  • Model participant-level random effects.
  • Represent more flexible covariance structures.
  • Avoid requiring sphericity for the repeated-measures outcome.

Mixed models do not automatically solve missing-data problems. Their validity depends on model assumptions, including assumptions about how data are missing.

Mixed models (later in semester) overcome two other significant flaws with RM ANOVAs.

Missing Data

Traditional repeated-measures ANOVA generally requires complete data for every participant across all measurement occasions. If a participant is missing even a single observation, many implementations remove that participant from the analysis.

For example, suppose a participant completes assessments at baseline and three months but misses the six-month assessment. In a traditional complete-case repeated-measures ANOVA, that participant would typically be excluded.

This approach, known as listwise deletion, can create several problems:

  • Reduced sample size and statistical power.
  • Potential bias if missingness is related to the outcome or predictors.
  • Loss of information from participants who completed most of the study.

Linear mixed models can often incorporate participants with incomplete data, provided the model and missing-data assumptions are reasonable.

Sphericity

Sphericity is an assumption concerning the variances of difference scores among the levels of a within-subjects factor.

For a study with three measurement occasions:

  • Baseline
  • Three months
  • Six months

the relevant difference scores include:

  • Baseline minus three months
  • Baseline minus six months
  • Three months minus six months

Sphericity requires the variances of these difference scores to be approximately equal.

Why Does Sphericity Matter?

Violations of sphericity can inflate the Type I error rate. This means that researchers may be more likely to conclude that a statistically significant effect exists when the effect is not actually present in the population.

Sphericity is relevant for within-subjects effects with three or more levels. With only two levels, there is only one set of difference scores, so sphericity is automatically satisfied.

Design Sphericity Required?
Pretest versus posttest No
Baseline, three months, six months Yes
Four listening conditions Yes

Testing Sphericity

The most common test of sphericity is Mauchly’s test.

The hypotheses are:

\[ H_0: \text{Sphericity is satisfied} \]

\[ H_A: \text{Sphericity is violated} \]

Interpretation is usually described as follows:

  • A nonsignificant result, \(p > .05\), provides no evidence against sphericity. It does not prove that the assumption is exactly true.
  • A significant result, \(p < .05\), indicates evidence that sphericity has been violated.

Because Mauchly’s test can be sensitive to sample size, it should be considered along with the design, the data, and the corrected results.

Adjusting for Violations of Sphericity

When sphericity is violated, researchers typically apply a correction to the degrees of freedom used in the F test.

Greenhouse-Geisser Correction

The Greenhouse-Geisser correction is commonly reported. It:

  • Reduces the degrees of freedom.
  • Produces a more conservative test.
  • Usually produces a larger p value.
  • Provides protection against inflated Type I error.

Huynh-Feldt Correction

The Huynh-Feldt correction is less conservative than the Greenhouse-Geisser correction. It adjusts the degrees of freedom to a lesser extent and is sometimes used when the violation is relatively mild.

Reporting Corrected Results

When Mauchly’s test indicates that sphericity has been violated, researchers should report the selected corrected test. For example:

Mauchly’s test indicated that the assumption of sphericity was violated. Therefore, Greenhouse-Geisser-corrected results were interpreted. The effect of time remained statistically significant, F(1.84, 53.24) = 8.72, p = .001.

The numerical values in this example are illustrative. Actual values should always be taken from the statistical output.

Analysis of Covariance

What Is ANCOVA?

ANCOVA, or analysis of covariance, combines ANOVA and regression. It allows researchers to compare group means while statistically controlling for one or more continuous variables called covariates.

This is a type of ANOVA focused on mean group differences across levels of a factor, but we also introduce other variables (often continuous) to control for their possible effects on the outcome.

The general ANCOVA model is:

\[ Y = \text{Group Effects} + \text{Covariate Effects} + \varepsilon \]

or:

\[ Y = X\beta + C\beta_C + \varepsilon \]

where:

  • \(X\beta\) represents the ANOVA portion of the model.
  • \(C\beta_C\) represents the regression portion of the model.
  • \(\varepsilon\) represents random error.

Why Use a Covariate?

A covariate can:

  • Explain variance in the dependent variable.
  • Reduce unexplained error variance.
  • Increase statistical precision.
  • Adjust for preexisting differences among participants.

ANCOVA evaluates whether group differences remain after statistically controlling for the covariate.

Interpreting ANCOVA

Unlike an unadjusted ANOVA, ANCOVA compares adjusted means, also called estimated marginal means.

Conceptually, ANCOVA asks:

What would the group differences look like if all groups had the same value on the covariate?

As a result, effects that appear significant in an unadjusted ANOVA may become nonsignificant after an important covariate is included.

Key Assumptions

Important ANCOVA assumptions include:

  1. The covariate has an approximately linear relationship with the dependent variable.
  2. The covariate is measured reliably.
  3. The relationship between the covariate and outcome is similar across groups.
  4. Observations are independent.
  5. Residuals are approximately normally distributed.
  6. Residual variances are reasonably similar across groups.
  7. There are no highly influential outliers.
  8. The covariate is measured before the outcome and is appropriate for adjustment.

The assumption that the regression relationship is the same across groups is called homogeneity of regression slopes. It can be evaluated by including factor-by-covariate interactions.

For example:

\[ Y = A + B + C + A \times B + A \times C + B \times C \]

where \(C\) is the covariate.

A significant factor-by-covariate interaction suggests that the relationship between the covariate and the outcome differs across groups. In that situation, a single adjusted group comparison may not adequately summarize the data.

Example 1: Mental Health Intervention for Latina Women

Suppose researchers evaluate a culturally tailored mental health intervention designed for Latina women experiencing psychological distress.

Independent Variable

  • Treatment condition:
    • Intervention
    • Control

Dependent Variables

  • Anxiety symptoms
  • PTSD symptoms
  • Depression symptoms

Covariates

  • Baseline anxiety
  • Baseline PTSD
  • Baseline depression

Participants complete measures before the intervention begins and again after the intervention concludes.

The primary research question is:

Do women receiving the intervention report lower anxiety, PTSD, and depression at posttest than women in the control group, after controlling for baseline symptom levels?

The ANCOVA model for anxiety is:

\[ \text{Posttest Anxiety} = \beta_0 + \beta_1(\text{Treatment}) + \beta_2(\text{Baseline Anxiety}) + \varepsilon \]

ANCOVA and Multiple Regression

ANCOVA is a linear regression model that includes categorical predictors and continuous covariates. The R syntax for ANCOVA and multiple regression is therefore the same.

Both analyses can address whether group mean differences remain after adjusting for a covariate. In experimental designs, the covariate is often a baseline score on the outcome.

In this example, baseline anxiety is not necessarily a nuisance variable. It may improve precision by accounting for individual differences that existed before treatment began.

By controlling for baseline scores, researchers obtain a more precise estimate of the treatment effect. However, the interpretation depends on the study design and on whether the covariate was measured before treatment.

Example 2: Generational Status and Breastfeeding Attitudes

Suppose researchers compare Latina mothers from three generational groups on attitudes toward breastfeeding. Some research suggests that acculturation among later-generation women may be associated with less positive views of breastfeeding and more favorable views of infant formula.

Family support of breastfeeding may also influence breastfeeding attitudes. Researchers therefore wish to determine whether generational differences remain after controlling for levels of family support.

Independent Variable

Generational status:

  • First-generation immigrant
  • Second-generation
  • Third-generation or later

Dependent Variable

  • Attitudes toward breastfeeding

Covariate

  • Family support

The research question is:

Do breastfeeding attitudes differ across generational groups after controlling for family support for breastfeeding?

The ANCOVA model is:

\[ \text{Breastfeeding Attitudes} = \beta_0 + \beta_1(\text{Generation}) + \beta_2(\text{Support}) + \varepsilon \]

In this example, support functions as an extraneous or explanatory variable because it may account for some differences in breastfeeding attitudes that are not attributable to generational status.

Simulation Study for ANCOVA

First-generation women are expected to report more positive breastfeeding attitudes than second-generation and third-generation-or-later women.
