Where We Have Been

Thus far we have compared means using t-tests.

Independent Samples t-Test:

  • Two independent groups
  • One continuous outcome

Examples:

  • Male vs Female
  • Treatment vs Control

Paired Samples t-Test:

  • Same participants measured twice
  • One continuous outcome

Examples:

  • Pretest vs Posttest
  • Before treatment vs After treatment

The Limitation of t-Tests

Suppose we want to compare:

  • Freshmen
  • Sophomores
  • Juniors
  • Seniors

Can we use a t-test?

No.

A t-test only compares:

\[2 \text{ groups}\]

Conducting multiple t-tests creates a problem:

Type I error inflation

The probability of making a false positive conclusion increases with each additional test.

Enter ANOVA

ANOVA stands for:

Analysis of Variance

Developed by:

Sir Ronald Fisher

Core question:

Are the observed group differences larger than would be expected due to random variation?

Instead of comparing two means directly, ANOVA compares:

  • Between-group variability

to

  • Within-group variability

The Logic of ANOVA

If groups truly differ:

  • Group means should be far apart

and

  • Individuals within groups should be relatively similar

ANOVA evaluates:

\[F = \frac{ \text{Between-Group Variance} }{ \text{Within-Group Variance} }\]

Understanding the F Ratio

Small F:

\[\text{Between} \approx \text{Within}\]

Little evidence of group differences.

Large F:

\[\text{Between} > \text{Within}\]

Evidence that group means differ.

Think of:

\[F = \frac{\text{Signal}}{\text{Noise}}\]

just as

\[t = \frac{\text{Signal}}{\text{Noise}}\]

for a t-test.

ANOVA Family Roadmap

We will study:

  1. One-Way ANOVA
  2. Factorial ANOVA
  3. Repeated Measures ANOVA
  4. Mixed ANOVA

Each model extends the previous one.

One-Way ANOVA

Research Question

Does exercise motivation differ across:

  • Freshmen
  • Sophomores
  • Juniors
  • Seniors

Independent Variable:

  • Class standing

Dependent Variable:

  • Exercise motivation

One categorical predictor

One continuous outcome

One-Way ANOVA Structure

\[Y = \mu + Group + Error\]

Where:

  • Y = outcome
  • μ = grand mean
  • Group = treatment effect
  • Error = residual variation

Question:

Are some group means different?

After One-Way ANOVA

A significant F-test tells us:

At least one group differs.

It does NOT tell us:

Which groups differ.

Follow-up analyses include:

  • Tukey HSD
  • Bonferroni tests
  • Planned contrasts

Factorial ANOVA

Now suppose we have:

  • Gender
  • Class Standing

and want to predict:

  • Exercise Motivation

We now have two independent variables.

Factorial ANOVA Questions

Main Effect of Gender:

Do males and females differ?

Main Effect of Class Standing:

Does motivation differ across class levels?

Interaction:

Does the effect of gender depend on class standing?

Interaction Effects

Main effects are often not the most interesting finding.

Interactions answer:

Does the impact of one variable change across levels of another variable?

Examples:

  • Treatment effectiveness differs by gender
  • Intervention effects differ by age
  • Program effects differ by ethnicity

Interactions are foundational for modern statistical modeling.

Repeated Measures ANOVA

Now suppose participants are measured:

  • Beginning of semester
  • Midterm
  • End of semester

Same participants repeatedly measured.

Question:

Does the outcome change across time?

Connection to Paired t-Tests

Paired t-Test:

\[2\]

time points

Repeated Measures ANOVA:

\[3+\]

time points

A paired t-test is simply a special case of repeated measures ANOVA.

Mixed ANOVA

Suppose we have:

Groups:

  • Treatment
  • Control

Measured at:

  • Pretest
  • Posttest
  • Follow-up

Now we have:

Between-Subjects Factor:

  • Group

Within-Subjects Factor:

  • Time

Mixed ANOVA Questions

Main Effect of Time:

Do scores change over time?

Main Effect of Group:

Do groups differ overall?

Interaction:

Do groups change differently over time?

The interaction is usually the primary research question.

ANCOVA

Analysis of Covariance

Research Question:

Do interventions differ after controlling for baseline differences?

Example:

Outcome:

  • Posttest depression

Group:

  • Treatment vs Control

Covariate:

  • Baseline depression

ANCOVA Model

ANCOVA combines:

ANOVA +

Regression

Conceptually:

\[Outcome = Group + Covariate + Error\]

ANCOVA adjusts group means for preexisting differences.

MANOVA

MANOVA stands for:

Multivariate Analysis of Variance

ANOVA:

  • One outcome

MANOVA:

  • Multiple outcomes

Example MANOVA

Independent Variable:

  • Treatment Group

Dependent Variables:

  • Depression
  • Anxiety
  • Stress
  • Self-Esteem

Question:

Do groups differ on the overall profile of outcomes?

Why Use MANOVA?

Instead of conducting:

  • ANOVA for Depression
  • ANOVA for Anxiety
  • ANOVA for Stress
  • ANOVA for Self-Esteem

MANOVA evaluates them simultaneously.

Advantages:

  • Controls Type I error
  • Examines combined outcome patterns

Linear Mixed Models

Repeated-measures ANOVA has limitations:

  • Missing data
  • Unequal measurement occasions
  • Unequal spacing of observations
  • Nested data structures

Mixed models address these issues.

Examples of Mixed Models

Students nested within classrooms

Repeated assessments nested within individuals

Patients nested within clinics

Family members nested within families

Longitudinal developmental studies

Mixed Model Example

\[Y_{ij} = \beta_0 + \beta_1(Time) + u_i + e_{ij}\]

Where:

  • Fixed effects describe average change
  • Random effects describe individual differences

Big Picture

t-Test

↓

One-Way ANOVA

↓

Factorial ANOVA

↓

Repeated Measures ANOVA

↓

Mixed ANOVA

↓

ANCOVA

↓

Key Takeaways

  • ANOVA extends the logic of the t-test.
  • One-Way ANOVA compares more than two groups.
  • Factorial ANOVA introduces multiple predictors and interactions.
  • Repeated Measures ANOVA analyzes change over time.
  • Mixed ANOVA combines groups and time.
  • ANCOVA adjusts for covariates.
  • MANOVA analyzes multiple outcomes simultaneously.
  • Mixed models provide a flexible framework for complex longitudinal and nested data.

Example 4: One-Way ANOVA

Research Question

Researchers were interested in whether ethnic-racial identity (ERI) exploration differed across stages of high school.

Specifically, they compared:

  • 9th Grade Students
  • 10th Grade Students
  • 11th Grade Students
  • 12th Grade Students

Outcome:

  • ERI Exploration Score

Research Question:

Does ethnic-racial identity exploration differ across grade levels?

Why Not Use Multiple t-Tests?

Suppose we compared:

  • 9th vs 10th
  • 9th vs 11th
  • 9th vs 12th
  • 10th vs 11th
  • 10th vs 12th
  • 11th vs 12th

That would require six separate t-tests.

Problems:

  • Increased Type I Error
  • More complicated interpretation

Instead, we use a single One-Way ANOVA.

Independent Variable

Grade Level

Group
9th
10th
11th
12th

This variable is categorical.

Dependent Variable

ERI Exploration

Example items:

  • I have spent time trying to learn more about my ethnic group.
  • I have participated in activities that help me understand my ethnic background.
  • I think a lot about what my ethnicity means to me.

Higher scores indicate greater exploration.

Hypotheses

Null Hypothesis:

\[H_0: \mu_{9th} = \mu_{10th} = \mu_{11th} = \mu_{12th}\]

All population means are equal.

Alternative Hypothesis:

\[H_A: \text{At least one population mean differs}\]

Notice that ANOVA does not specify which group differs.

Sample Data

Suppose the study produced the following sample means.

Grade N Mean
9th 50 2.90
10th 50 3.12
11th 50 3.47
12th 50 3.61

Visual inspection suggests exploration may increase across grade levels.

The question is:

Are these differences larger than we would expect from sampling error alone?

Logic of ANOVA

ANOVA compares:

Between-Group Variation

Differences among group means

versus

Within-Group Variation

Differences among individuals inside each group

The test statistic is

\[F= \frac{ MS_{Between} }{ MS_{Within} }\]

Understanding the F Ratio

If group means are very similar:

\[F \approx 1\]

Little evidence of group differences.

If group means differ substantially:

\[F > 1\]

Evidence of group differences.

Larger F values provide stronger evidence against the null hypothesis.

What Does the Omnibus Test Tell Us?

The ANOVA tells us:

✅ Some mean differs

The ANOVA does NOT tell us:

❌ Which means differ

To answer that question we conduct post hoc comparisons.

APA Style Write-Up

A one-way analysis of variance examined differences in ethnic-racial identity exploration across grade levels. Results indicated a significant effect of grade level on exploration, F(3, 196) = 21.84, p < .001. Follow-up Tukey comparisons suggested that students in the upper grades reported greater levels of ethnic-racial identity exploration than students in the lower grades.

Key Takeaways

  • One-Way ANOVA compares three or more means.
  • The F-test compares between-group variability to within-group variability.
  • A significant omnibus test indicates that at least one mean differs.
  • Post hoc tests identify where differences occur.
  • Effect sizes quantify the magnitude of group differences.
