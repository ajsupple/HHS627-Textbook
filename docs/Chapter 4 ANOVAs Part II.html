ANOVAs Part II

Thus far we considered an ANOVA with three groups and one outcome. That model was a One-Way ANOVA because it included one factor (language/cultural brokering) and one outcome. We are frequently going to want to consider more complex models that include multiple outcomes, comparing within-person scores that are repeated over time, more than one factor in a study, and including covariates of control variables.

Factorial ANOVA

Factorial ANOVAs are models with more than one factor. These models offer the ability to consider mean differences across multiple factors simultaneously (the main effects) and the interaction between factors.

NoteWhat is a Factor?

A factor refers to a categorical variable and is often considered the independent variable (IV) in an ANOVA. In ANOVA, we examine whether the means of the dependent variable differ across the levels of one or more factors. Models with one factor are called one-way ANOVAs, models with two factors are called two-way ANOVAs, and more generally ANOVAs with two or more factors are referred to as factorial ANOVAs.

Factorial ANOVAs are often described by the number of levels in each factor. For example, a two-way ANOVA with two binary factors would be called a 2 × 2 ANOVA. An ANOVA with three factors having two, three, and two levels, respectively, would be called a 2 × 3 × 2 ANOVA.

In R, it is important to distinguish between categorical and numeric independent variables. For ANOVA, categorical predictors should generally be coded as factors. When a variable is coded as a factor, values such as 1, 2, and 3 or labels such as “White”, “Black”, and “Latino” are treated as distinct levels of a categorical variable. If the variable is instead treated as numeric, R assumes the values represent a quantitative scale and estimates a linear relationship rather than separate group means. This changes the model being estimated and can affect the associated degrees of freedom and statistical tests.

Let’s work through an example where we might be interested in self-reported anxiety scores on adolescent males based on sexual orientation and race. This example will have two factors and so is referred to as a Two-Way Analysis of Variance.

The two factors are:

  • Race: where participants indicated whether they were African American/Black or European American/White
  • Sexual orientation: where participants indicated if they were gay or straight

This analysis would have two factors (race and sexual orientation) and one outcome. Each variable has two levels, resulting in four possible groups.

This design allows for a few different research questions:

  1. Is self-reported anxiety higher among gay adolescent males compared to straight males?
  2. Is self-reported anxiety higher among White adolescent males compared to Black males?
  3. Is the difference in anxiety among gay/straight males greater for Black males compared to white males?

The first two of these research questions test for main effects, while the third question tests an interaction effect.

NoteInteraction Effects

Interaction effects are sometimes referred to as moderator effects. Moderators in general refer to variables that modify the association of a predictor on an outcome. In ANOVAs, the interaction can be thought of as group means varying across one factor in a different way based on the other factor. A significant interaction would suggest that the main factor difference depends upon levels of the other factor.

A 2 X 2 ANOVA Comparing Anxiety Scores by Race and Sexual Orientation

We will start with an example study, work through some research questions, prepare some data to analyze, then use R to run the 2 X 2 ANOVA and interpret our results.

  • Possible range: 10 to 28

Independent Variables

  • Race

    • White
  • Sexual Orientation
    • Gay
    • Straight

Study Groups

  1. Black and Gay
  2. Black and Straight
  3. White and Gay

The row marginal means show the average anxiety score for each Race group. Black adolescents have a higher mean anxiety score (M = 17.1) than White adolescents (M = 15.4), suggesting a possible main effect of Race.

Similarly, the column marginal means show that gay adolescents (M = 17.42) report higher anxiety than straight adolescents (M = 15.09), suggesting a possible main effect of Sexual Orientation.

In this particular instance, there appears to be an interaction that qualifies these differences. For example, by “holding sexual orientation constant” in the column “straight”, we see a very small anxiety difference by race. Another way to think about this is that if we look within race groups, we might see a somewhat modest difference in anxiety when comparing gay (\(M\) = 16.48) to straight White adolescents (\(M\) = 14.42) but a large difference when comparing gay (\(M\) = 18.25) to straight adolescents (\(M\) = 15.066) who are Black. That would suggest the higher anxiety “effect” of being gay would be stronger among Black adolescents when compared to that same “effect” for White adolescents.

We would test that with the 2 X 2 ANOVA

The Factorial ANOVA Model Details

The two-way ANOVA can be represented as:

\[Y = \mu + \text{Race} + \text{Orientation} + \text{Race} \times \text{Orientation} + e\] Partitioning Variability into Sources for Race, Orientation and the Interaction

As with a one-way ANOVA, total variability is divided into explained (between groups) and unexplained (within-groups) components.

\[SS_{Total} = SS_{Race} + SS_{Orientation} + SS_{Race\times Orientation} + SS_{Error}\]

The ANOVA table therefore evaluates:

  1. Main effect of race
  2. Main effect of sexual orientation
  3. Race × Sexual Orientation interaction
  4. Residual error (what isn’t explained by the factors)

Degrees of Freedom

For this example:

\[df_{Race}=2-1=1\]

\[df_{Orientation}=2-1=1\]

\[df_{Interaction}=(2-1)(2-1)=1\]

\[df_{Error}=N-ab\]

where:

  • \(N=60\)
  • \(a=2\) Race groups
  • \(b=2\) Orientation groups

Therefore,

\[df_{Error}=60-4=56\]

Mean Squares

\[MS_{Race} = \frac{SS_{Race}} {df_{Race}}\]

\[MS_{Orientation} = \frac{SS_{Orientation}} {df_{Orientation}}\]

\[MS_{Interaction} = \frac{SS_{Interaction}} {df_{Interaction}}\]

\[MS_{Error} = \frac{SS_{Error}} {df_{Error}}\]

F Statistics

For each effect, the variability explained by that effect is compared with the unexplained within-group variability. Put another way, each effect is focused on how much difference there is across groups relative to how much “error” which is the within-group differences.

Main Effect of Race

\[F_{Race} = \frac{MS_{Race}} {MS_{Error}}\]

Main Effect of Sexual Orientation

\[F_{Orientation} = \frac{MS_{Orientation}} {MS_{Error}}\]

Interaction Effect

\[F_{Interaction} = \frac{MS_{Interaction}} {MS_{Error}}\]

Warning

In unbalanced factorial designs, Type I (sequential) sums of squares can produce different conclusions depending on the order in which factors enter the model. In this example, Race is statistically significant when entered first (p = .044) but not when entered after Orientation (p = .087). This occurs because the factors share explanatory variance, and the first factor entered receives credit for that shared variance.

Type II Sum of Squares fixes tests this differently and doesn’t depend on the order of entry for the factors. Type II SS tests each main effect controlling for the other, but those main effects are not adjusted for the interaction. In cases where the interaction is absent or not of interest, this test is appropriate.

NoteDummy versus Effects Coding

Dummy coding is the default in R with factor variables. Typically in a factor variable there is a level of 0. That level becomes a default, against which other means are compared. In the context of ANOVAs, dummy coding makes main effects difficult to interpret in the context of interaction effects. With dummy coding, a main effect of one factor is interpreted as a group difference in that factor when the other factor is 0 or for the reference group.

For our example, the significant main effect indicating a race difference in anxiety would be comparing only among the straight adolescents. The main effect of sexual orientation, likewise, would represent mean differences by sexual orientation among the white participants. In the context of a statistically significant interaction, we would generally avoid interpreting these main effects and instead, compute marginal means or plot means to understand the nature of the interaction.

Effects coding assigns numerical values to factor levels that sum to zero. For a two-group race variable, for example, Black participants might be coded as -1 and White participants as +1. Unlike dummy coding, which compares groups at a specific reference category, effects coding centers the factors on the grand mean. In a 2 × 2 ANOVA, the main effect of Race therefore represents the average racial difference across sexual orientation groups, and the main effect of Sexual Orientation represents the average orientation difference across racial groups. When an interaction is present, these main effects can still be interpreted as average effects, although the interaction indicates that the size of the group differences varies across levels of the other factor.

NoteType I, II, and III Sum of Squares summary
  • Factorial ANOVAs can be balanced or unbalanced
    • Balanced is when the groups have the same sample size in all of the cells
    • Unbalanced is when groups have different sample sizes in the cells
      • There are different ways to do sum of squares tests in ANOVAs
      • Some statistical packages by default use Type III Sum of Squares
      • In cases where you have unbalanced groups, Type III can help with interpretation of main effects

Type I (Sequential) sums of squares evaluate effects in the order they are entered into the model. Results may change if the order of variables changes. Type II sums of squares test each main effect after adjusting for the other main effects but ignore interactions when testing main effects. Type III sums of squares test each effect after adjusting for all other effects in the model, including interactions.

Because Type I tests depend on variable order, they are most appropriate for hierarchical models or balanced designs. In contrast, Type III sums of squares are commonly used in unbalanced factorial ANOVAs when interactions are included in the model.

Type III asks, “What does this variable explain after accounting for everything else?

More Complex Factorial ANOVAs

Example: Examining body image in girls aged 8-11 versus 12-15 across four different types of sports played.

  • Body image assessed by items related to “I feel good about my body”, “I am comfortable in my body”, “I take a positive attitude toward my body”
    • 5-point Likert scale from Never to Always
    • 10 items added together for scores ranging from 10 to 50
  • Sample of girls who frequently participated in either (competitively, one sport only) ice skating, gymnastics, soccer, or basketball
    • Gymnastics and ice skating are judged sports which may have stronger effects on body image in girls, compared to team sports like soccer or basketball
    • Age groupings of 8-11 year old girls versus 12-15 year old girls to roughly capture the period of pubertal transition when developmental changes in body image may occur more frequently

Hypotheses

  • Younger girls will report higher average body image compared to older girls
  • Girls who play basketball and soccer will report higher average body image than will girls who participate in skating and gymnastics
  • **There will be an interaction, however, so that body image differences by age will be more pronounced in the two judged sports of ice skating and gymnastics.

In the context of an interaction effect, it is helpful to look at marginal means to get a sense of the differences.

Then we can also examine simple effects which are differences by one factor, but within the other factor. So for example, we can look at age differences within each sport to test for statistically significant differences. NoteEstimated Marginal Means with the emmeans Package

emmeans is an R package that has functions for calculating marginal means by using model results. These can be used in ANOVAs to show what averages are for certain groups when adjusting for other variables in the model.

In factorial ANOVA, emmeans is particularly useful because the ANOVA table indicates whether effects are statistically significant, but it does not identify which groups differ or provide estimates of group means. The emmeans package allows researchers to:

  • Obtain estimated marginal means for factors in the model.
  • Conduct pairwise comparisons among group means.
  • Examine simple effects following a significant interaction.
  • Test theory-driven planned contrasts.
  • Compute confidence intervals and effect estimates.
NotePlanned Contrasts

Planned contrasts involve a way of coding factors so that there are specific comparisons being made across groups in an ANOVA. These generally were used to make a specific comparison within multiple grouping, e.g., is any therapy (CBT, DBT, talk, recreational) better than none?

The benefits of a planned contrast is that researchers do not need to examine a global F test, can test targeted differences, and these have greater statistical power. Planned contrasts are a priori tests as contrasted to post hoc tests like Tukey HSD and others.

Multivariate Analysis of Variance (MANOVA)

What is MANOVA?

Multivariate Analysis of Variance (MANOVA) is an extension of ANOVA that allows researchers to examine group differences on multiple dependent variables simultaneously.

Recall that ANOVA asks:

Do groups differ on a single outcome?

MANOVA asks:

Do groups differ on a combination of outcomes?

For example, rather than examining whether language brokering groups differ only in self-esteem, a MANOVA can simultaneously evaluate group differences in:

  • Self-esteem
  • Family cohesion
  • Depressive symptoms

Because these outcomes are related, analyzing them together can provide a more complete understanding of group differences.

When is MANOVA Used?

MANOVA is appropriate when:

  1. There are one or more categorical independent variables.
  2. There are multiple continuous dependent variables.
  3. The dependent variables are conceptually related.
  4. Researchers want to control the overall Type I error rate.

Examples include:

  • Comparing ethnic groups on multiple parenting dimensions.
  • Comparing intervention groups on several psychosocial outcomes.
  • Comparing family structures on several indicators of child well-being.

Why Not Run Multiple ANOVAs?

Researchers could conduct separate ANOVAs for each dependent variable.

However, doing so creates two concerns.

  • Inflation of Type I Error

    • Each hypothesis test introduces a chance of a false positive.
    • Running several ANOVAs increases the likelihood of finding at least one significant result due to chance alone.

  • Ignoring Relationships Among Variables

Many HHS outcomes may be related, for example if comparing groups on depression, anxiety, and stress, these are related constructs and MANOVA can incorporate those relationships into the analysis.

What Does MANOVA Test?

MANOVA first evaluates whether groups differ on the combined set of dependent variables.

The null hypothesis is:

\[H_0:\text{ The vector of population means is equal across groups}\] - The vector refers to the totality of means across the various outcome variables under consideration. So the main null hypotheses is that those means are equal across the groups. Rejecting that null would indicate at least some differences in some outcomes across some groups.

If the overall MANOVA is significant, researchers typically examine:

  • Follow-up ANOVAs
  • Estimated marginal means
  • Pairwise comparisons

to determine which dependent variables contribute to the overall multivariate effect.

  • Note that multivariate refers to multiple outcomes

Multivariate Test Statistics

When conducting a MANOVA, the overall test examines whether the groups differ on the combined dependent variables. Four common test statistics are available:

  1. Pillai’s Trace
    • Range: 0 to number of DVs
    • Interpretation: Larger values indicate stronger multivariate effects
    • Robustness: Most robust to violations of assumptions (especially homogeneity of covariance matrices)
    • Best used when sample sizes are unequal or assumptions are violated
  2. Wilks’ Lambda
    • Range: 0 to 1 (inverse interpretation—smaller values indicate stronger effects)
    • Interpretation: Smaller values indicate stronger multivariate effects
    • Power: Generally most powerful test
    • Assumption sensitive: More sensitive to violations of homogeneity assumption
    • Best used when assumptions are met and sample sizes are equal
  3. Hotelling’s Trace
    • Similar interpretation to Pillai’s Trace (larger values = stronger effects)
    • Power: Similar power to Wilks’ Lambda
    • Best for: Two-group comparisons or when homogeneity assumptions are met
  4. Roy’s Criterion (Greatest Root)
    • Range: 0 to infinity
    • Interpretation: Larger values indicate stronger effects
    • Power: Most powerful when the first discriminant function accounts for most of the variance
    • Assumption sensitive: Most sensitive to violations of assumptions
    • Best for: Confirmatory studies with clear expectations

In our example, we used Pillai’s Trace test, which is a conservative and robust choice for introductory MANOVA. The interpretation is straightforward: Pillai’s Trace indicates a multivariate effect of levels of culture-based conflicts with parents on the combined psychosocial outcomes.

MANOVA Assumptions

Like ANOVA, MANOVA is based on several key assumptions, including the independence of observations. The other MANOVA assumptions are a bit more complicated due to the fact that MANOVA has multiple outcome variables and is, as such, multivariate.

So for example, instead of normality of the outcomes, MANOVA is also concerned with:

  1. multivariate normality

    • The dependent variables should jointly follow a multivariate normal distribution within each group. This means that all linear combinations of the dependent variables should be normally distributed within each group. While MANOVA is somewhat robust to violations of this assumption with large samples, severe violations should be addressed (e.g., through transformation or non-parametric alternatives).

  2. Homogeneity of Variance-Covariance Matrices

    • The variance-covariance matrices of the dependent variables should be approximately equal across all groups. This is tested using Box’s M test. Unlike univariate ANOVA which tests homogeneity of variance (Levene’s test), MANOVA tests the homogeneity of the entire covariance matrix structure.

  3. Linearity

    • The relationship between all pairs of dependent variables should be linear within each group. Scatterplots between pairs of DVs can be examined to assess this.

  4. Absence of Multicollinearity Among Dependent Variables

    • The dependent variables should not be so highly correlated that they are essentially measuring the same construct (multicollinearity), nor should there be a perfect linear relationship (singularity). Generally, correlations between DVs should be moderate (r < .90).

  5. Adequate Sample Size

    • MANOVA requires sufficient sample size to reliably estimate the covariance matrices. A common rule of thumb is that each group should have more cases than the number of dependent variables. For example, with three dependent variables, each group should ideally have at least 20 participants.

Testing Homogeneity of Variance-Covariance Matrices: Box’s M Test

The most commonly tested MANOVA assumption is homogeneity of variance-covariance matrices. While univariate ANOVA uses Levene’s test to evaluate equality of variances across groups, MANOVA uses Box’s M test.

A non-significant Box’s M test (p > .05) suggests that the covariance matrices are approximately equal across groups, which satisfies the assumption. However, Box’s M is sensitive to violations of multivariate normality, so a significant result doesn’t necessarily invalidate MANOVA results, especially with larger sample sizes. In practice, if sample sizes are equal across groups and reasonably large, moderate violations of this assumption are often tolerable.
